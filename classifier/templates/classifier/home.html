Teaching a network to read handwriting

This page is the write-up: how we built a convolutional network that reads 28×28 grayscale digits, what worked, what didn't, and where it still gets confused. Try the classifier on your own digit once you've read this.

The approach

We started with a CNN that used two convolutional layers, with 32 and 64 filters, followed by pooling, a dense layer, and dropout. We then tried a deeper version with two convolutional layers in each block, batch normalization, and dropout. We scaled the grayscale pixel values from 0–255 to 0–1 and used a validation set to compare the models during training. The improved model performed better, so we saved it as our final classifier. On the separate 10,000-image test set, it reached 99.59% accuracy.

tip that saved us real time: keep the trained model and its confusion matrix in memory before closing the notebook — regenerating both means retraining from scratch.

What worked, what didn't

Our first CNN gave us a useful starting point. Adding a second convolution to each block, batch normalization, and dropout improved the model we selected for final testing. Watching validation accuracy and loss helped us decide which saved version to use. The model still made mistakes on some unusual handwritten digits, so adding more training data that resembles those difficult examples would be worth testing next.

Where the model succeeds and struggles

The model correctly classified 9,959 of the 10,000 test images and misclassified 41. The figures below show examples of both results. Each label gives the true digit, the predicted digit, and the model's confidence in its prediction.

The confusion matrix shows that errors were rare and spread across several digits. The most frequent mix-up was a handwritten 5 read as a 3, which happened four times. The model also read a 6 as a 5, a 9 as a 4, and a 7 as a 2 three times each. These digits can share similar curves or strokes, especially when handwriting is faint, incomplete, or unusually shaped. Some wrong predictions had high confidence, reminding us that confidence alone does not guarantee a correct answer.

Will we ever hit 100%?

We do not expect 100% accuracy on every new handwritten digit. Handwriting varies, and some images are faint, incomplete, or difficult even for a person to read. Our model correctly classified 9,959 of 10,000 test images, but the remaining 41 show that similar-looking digits can still cause errors. More examples of difficult handwriting might improve performance, but a perfect result on one test set would not guarantee perfect predictions on future images.
